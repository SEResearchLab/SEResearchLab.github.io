01

Motivation

Agents now open a measurable share of pull requests in open-source projects, but code review was designed around a human author who can be asked what they intended.

We do not yet know how reviewers reallocate attention when that conversation is unavailable — or what it costs them.

02

Research questions

RQ1

How do reviewers allocate attention when the author of a change is an agent rather than a colleague?

RQ2

Which review signals substitute for the missing author rationale?

RQ3

What does the substitution cost in time and defect detection?

03

Contributions

A mixed-methods account of reviewer attention under agent authorship, grounded in 1,204 pull requests and 12 follow-up interviews.

An instrument others can reuse: the coding scheme, the think-aloud protocol and the analysis scripts are in the replication package.

Three design implications for review tooling, stated concretely enough to be disagreed with.

06

Interpretation

Labelling the author changes behaviour more than the code does — the same diff is read differently once its provenance is visible.

Reviewers substitute re-reading for asking. That recovers defect detection but costs roughly six minutes per change.

07

Threats to validity

Construct: self-reported effort may lag observed effort; we triangulate with timestamps.

External: OSS projects with public review; findings may not transfer to closed teams with different norms.

Internal: labelling was not randomised in two repositories.

08

Conclusion & next steps

Provenance is a review signal, not metadata. Tooling that hides it is making a decision on the reviewer's behalf.

Next: a field experiment varying how rationale is surfaced, running with two industrial partners in 2027.
